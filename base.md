## Privacy

Privacy can come in many forms, but at it's core it only means one thing: control over to whom you share information about you. When I talk about losing privacy I mean losing control over your life. Maybe you want to tell you mother how much you love her, but not the goverment. That is your and your decision only. Of course, ethics are not black and white, in some cases one right rules over the other. For example, in most countries in the world, it is evident that the police can breach this privacy to protect its citizens. It just comes down to the what, who, how and when questions.

The Internet wasn't promised to be a big markeplace. I'm not talking about shops trying to sell you a new dishwasher. No. I'm talking about the monetization of data, information and intelligence. Even if you don't do anything on the Internet, you still generate data, with your phone connected to it. The question is, who has this data? Well, your service provider has it, like if you are using Facebook or Instagram, they definitely have it. Also the government can access this data, there is no technological obstacle there, only laws made by bureocrats, but hey, don't worry the government would never hurt your rights. From these two big players, the data can really travel to data brokers, to another third party or even the secret service. Let's not talk about how can this data be used yet, but focus on controlling the places the data spreads.

Keep it simple, you are talking with your friend on a mainstream messagin app. Who do you trust with these messages? Would you publicly share it with anyone? Probaly no. Whenever you send a message, on most normie applications, it works by a client-server model, meaning that your client, like your phone, send the message first to a server maintained by the service provider, and then the server will forward it the recipent's cient. This is done so the two communicating don't have to be online all the time to recieve messages. The server stores and forwards, because it is always online. Common network protocols nowadays implement encryption layers, which are cryptographically proven to guarantee that only the two network devices, in this case the server and your client, can actually understand the messages. I won't go into the details of this. However without any further transformations, the server can still read the message. Your soyware application like Facebook, Gmail, Instagram, Snapchat and so on does exactly that. Whatever you send, you don't have control over it.

We can't really trust the server, server administrators can be bribed, blackmailed and the companies holding these servers really don't care about privacy at all, as long as that's not their whole business modell, like for Signal. In math we trust, so researchers built protocols made out of cryptographically secure primitives, for secure encrypted communications between clients. This way, the server can only see random gibberish, and only the sender and the recipient actually see the messages. We call this communication end-to-end-encrypted or as you may see it, E2EE. In this scenario, it doesn't matter if the server has malicious intent, the universe would go cold faster, than they could break the encryption behind it.

We have E2EE, now we are all safe now right? Not exactly, we still have to trust, and take the word of the service providers, that they actually implement a secure E2EE protocol without any backdoors for nation states, companies or else. One way, they could prove that there is no sketchy stuff in their application, if they open sourced it and made the whole messaging transparent so anyone, including security researchers, would be able to make sure that indeed it is secure. But guess what? That is not the case. Most companies are very closed source, because they would like to keep their code base as a secret, from their competitors I guess. When you use closed source, you are betting that your company is better in security than the whole world's security community. That's a big gamble. Even if the company is not evil to the bone, they would still comply with goverment inquiries.

There is no universal solution to privacy-reserving communication, since the question itself is complex, and have multiple parameters. What's the best application out there to communicate privately? It depends. There are multiple solutions which are suck way less than the mainstream applications used by the general populus. In cybersecurity the term <i>threat modell</i> is commonly used for assessing what an attacker is capable of with plausible reason. Based on this threat modell, multiple risks come out of the box, and a defender has to match linearly the risks with the amount of effort put into the counter measures. For example in communication, your grandma and a political activist or journalist would have a way different threat modell. However, I think we should not forget that privacy is a non-negoitable human right. It's not like your grandma should have less privacy, just because there is a smaller chance that someone else would try to breach that privacy. This is true especially in the world we live in, where untargeted surveillance is always a returning topic. I won't compare private communication methods, since there are already plenty of better said comparisons out there (example), but I will argue why I started using XMPP. By the way, nice try feds, but communication with my buddies is already maximum encrypted by being inside of my head.

Okay, maybe the company does makes its protocol open and transparent, like in the case of Signal. Good. Then we have Chat Control.
